Observability is the property of being able to ask a running system new questions without shipping new code. Metrics are cheap aggregated counters and timers, ideal for dashboards and alerts but blind to individual requests. Traces follow one request across every service it touches, turning a mystery about aggregate latency into a picture of exactly where one slow request spent its time. Logs carry the arbitrary detail the other two abandon. None of the three is sufficient alone, and the craft is in wiring them together, so an alert on a metric leads to the traces behind it and each trace leads to its logs.

An incident begins when the system stops meeting its promises and ends, operationally, when it meets them again. Everything between is triage: mitigate first, understand later. The instinct to diagnose the root cause while users are down is strong and usually wrong, because rollback, failover, and shedding load restore service faster than insight does. Understanding belongs to the review that follows, and the review is worth little unless it is blameless. A team that punishes the engineer nearest the failure teaches everyone to hide near misses, and hidden near misses are how the same failure returns at a worse hour.

Authentication asks who you are; authorization asks what you may do. Confusing the two produces systems that check identity carefully at the front door and then let anyone who entered wander every room. Passwords survive only as salted, slow hashes, so that a stolen database yields guesses rather than credentials. Tokens carry proof of identity between services, and their expiry is the quiet safety property: a leaked credential that dies in minutes is an incident, while one that lives forever is a breach. Secrets belong in a manager built for them, never in source control, and every credential should have a rotation story before it has a production deployment.

Capacity planning is the argument between what the system can do and what will be asked of it. Load testing supplies the first number, and the honest version measures the knee of the curve: the point where latency stops rising gently and begins to climb without bound as queues form. Forecasting supplies the second number and is always wrong in detail, so the plan is really a margin. Autoscaling narrows the argument by renting capacity as demand arrives, but it reacts on a delay, and a spike faster than the reaction time still lands on whatever headroom was already running.

Technical debt is the gap between the design the system has and the design its current job requires. Some debt is taken deliberately, a shortcut chosen with open eyes to reach a deadline, and some accumulates silently as requirements drift away from assumptions the code still enforces. The interest payments are concrete: every feature near the debt costs more, reviews slow down, and the number of people willing to touch the module shrinks toward one. Paying it down competes against visible feature work, which is why the argument for it must be made in the currency of delivery speed rather than aesthetics.
